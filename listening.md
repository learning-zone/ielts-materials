# IELTS General Training Listening

<br>

## Overview

The IELTS Listening test is 30 minutes long (+ 10 minutes transfer time in paper-based IELTS).  
It consists of **4 sections** with **40 questions** total.

| Section | Context | Speakers | Focus |
|---------|---------|----------|-------|
| Section 1 | Everyday / Social | 2 people (conversation) | Practical information (names, dates, addresses) |
| Section 2 | Everyday / Social | 1 person (monologue) | Descriptions, instructions, announcements |
| Section 3 | Academic / Training | 2–4 people (discussion) | Opinions, plans, academic tasks |
| Section 4 | Academic / Training | 1 person (lecture) | Complex ideas, academic content |

## Section 4 — Lecture: The History of Paper

> **Listening Passage — Transcript**
>
> Good morning everyone, and welcome to today\'s lecture. We'll be looking at something that most of us take entirely for granted — paper — and tracing its remarkable journey from ancient China to the digital age.
>
> Paper was first invented in China around **105** AD, traditionally credited to a court official named Cai Lun, who worked in the imperial court of the Han Dynasty. However, archaeological evidence suggests that cruder forms of paper existed even earlier, from around the second century BC. The key material used in early Chinese papermaking was **mulberry** bark, which was soaked, beaten, and pressed into thin sheets. Hemp fibres and rags were also used, but mulberry was the dominant material in the classical period.
>
> Paper-making knowledge spread westward gradually — first to Korea, then to Japan. It reached the Arab world through **prisoners of war** captured during the Battle of Talas in 751 AD, a pivotal conflict between Tang Dynasty China and the Abbasid Caliphate. Arab scholars and craftspeople quickly adopted and refined the technique, and for several centuries the Islamic world was the primary producer of high-quality paper in the western hemisphere.
>
> European production of paper began in **Spain** in the twelfth century, introduced through the Moorish civilisation of Al-Andalus. From there, it spread gradually northward through Italy, France, and Germany. The quality of European paper improved substantially over the following centuries as new techniques were adopted and refined.
>
> The invention of the **printing press** by Gutenberg around 1440 was arguably the single most transformative moment in the history of paper demand. Almost overnight, the appetite for paper expanded exponentially as books, pamphlets, and documents could be produced in quantities that were previously unimaginable.
>
> In the modern era, paper is mainly made from **wood pulp** — a dramatic departure from the rag-based papers of the medieval period. Softwood trees such as spruce and pine are the primary raw materials. The environmental implications of this are significant, and the industry has faced considerable pressure to adopt more sustainable practices.
>
> On that note — recycled paper reduces water use in the manufacturing process by up to **60** percent compared to virgin paper production, which is a considerable saving given the enormous quantities of water involved in traditional papermaking.
>
> Today, the largest paper-producing country in the world is **China**, which accounts for approximately a quarter of global production, followed by the United States and Japan.
>
> As we all know, digital documents have reduced **office** paper use significantly over the past two decades. However, the much-predicted "paperless office" has not fully materialised — global paper consumption has actually remained surprisingly resilient, particularly in developing economies.
>
> Finally, looking ahead — researchers are now developing paper made from **agricultural waste** — materials such as sugarcane fibre and wheat straw — which may offer a genuinely sustainable alternative to wood-based paper in the coming decades. I find this one of the more exciting material science developments in a field that has been relatively stable for centuries.

*Listen to a university lecture on the invention and spread of paper-making.*

## Section 4 — Lecture: Coral Reef Ecosystems

> **Listening Passage — Transcript**
>
> Good morning. Today we continue our series on marine biology with a focus on one of the planet\'s most extraordinary and endangered ecosystems — coral reefs.
>
> Let me begin with some context. Coral reefs cover less than **one percent** of the ocean floor, yet they support around twenty-five percent of all marine species. That staggering disproportion tells you everything about their ecological importance — they are arguably the most productive ecosystems on Earth relative to their area.
>
> The main threat facing reefs globally is **bleaching** — a process caused by rising sea temperatures. When water temperatures rise even slightly above the normal range — often by just one to two degrees Celsius — the symbiotic algae that live inside coral tissues and provide them with nutrition are expelled. Without these algae, the coral turns white — hence "bleaching" — and if the stress continues, the coral will die. This process is known as coral **bleaching** and has devastated reef systems worldwide, most notably the Great Barrier Reef, which has experienced multiple mass bleaching events in recent years.
>
> Other major threats include **destructive** fishing practices — the use of dynamite and cyanide to catch fish — and coastal **development**, which generates sediment runoff that smothers reefs and reduces water clarity. Pollution from agricultural runoff also contributes significantly.
>
> Scientists use **satellite** monitoring to track reef health at a global scale — this has transformed our ability to detect early warning signs of stress and respond more rapidly. At a more granular level, underwater surveys and temperature sensors provide detailed data on specific reef systems.
>
> In terms of restoration, the two most promising methods currently in use are **coral** transplanting — growing coral fragments in nurseries and attaching them to damaged reefs — and the use of **artificial** structures, such as specially designed reef modules, which provide new substrate for coral larvae to settle on.
>
> The economic dimension is also significant. The Great Barrier Reef alone generates approximately **6** billion dollars annually for the Australian economy through tourism, fishing, and related industries — making reef conservation not merely an environmental imperative but an economic one.
>
> Finally, international conservation efforts are coordinated under frameworks such as the **Biodiversity** Convention, which provides the legal and policy architecture for national governments to develop and implement reef protection strategies.

## Section 4 — Lecture: Psychology of Decision Making

> **Listening Passage — Transcript**
>
> Good morning, everyone. Today\'s lecture explores the psychology behind how we make decisions — a topic that sits at the intersection of cognitive science, economics, and behavioural science.
>
> We'll start with Daniel Kahneman\'s dual-process theory, arguably the most influential framework in the field. Kahneman distinguishes between two modes of thinking. System 1 is **fast and intuitive** — it operates automatically, requires little conscious effort, and relies on mental shortcuts or "heuristics." System 2, by contrast, is **slow and deliberate** — it\'s analytical, effortful, and engaged when we face complex or unfamiliar problems.
>
> The key insight is that most of our daily decisions are made by System 1 — often without us being aware of it. This makes us efficient, but also vulnerable to predictable errors in judgment.
>
> One of the most well-documented biases is **anchoring**. When making a numerical estimate or decision, we are disproportionately influenced by the first **number or figure** we encounter, even if it\'s entirely arbitrary. Classic experiments have shown that people\'s salary negotiations, house price estimations, and even criminal sentencing recommendations are all affected by irrelevant initial numbers.
>
> The **availability heuristic** leads us to overweight information that is **recent or memorable**. We judge the likelihood of events based on how easily examples come to mind — which is why vivid news stories can distort our risk perception far beyond actual statistical probabilities.
>
> **Confirmation bias** is our tendency to seek and favour information that **confirms** our existing beliefs, while discounting contradictory evidence. This has profound implications for scientific reasoning, political discourse, and even clinical medicine.
>
> Kahneman\'s research also demonstrated a powerful asymmetry: people are consistently more strongly motivated to avoid **losses** than to acquire equivalent gains. This is known as loss aversion, and it influences everything from financial decisions to policy compliance.
>
> The policy applications of this work are substantial. **Nudge theory**, associated with **Thaler** and Sunstein, proposes that we can improve decision-making by redesigning the **choice or decision** architecture — the environment in which decisions are made — without removing freedom of choice. Governments have applied nudges to encourage **financial** saving, pension enrolment, and organ donation, achieving significant behavioural change at minimal cost.
>
> Critics argue, however, that nudges undermine **individual** autonomy by manipulating rather than informing. Looking ahead, researchers are increasingly focused on the role of **artificial intelligence and digital** platforms in shaping decision environments — a frontier with enormous ethical implications.

>

## Section 4 — Lecture: The Human Microbiome

> **Listening Passage — Transcript**
>
> Good morning, everyone. Today we're exploring one of the fastest-growing fields in biomedical science — the human microbiome.
>
> The human body contains approximately **37 trillion** microbial cells — comparable in number to the body\'s own cells. The vast majority of these microbes reside in the gut, particularly the large intestine.
>
> The gut microbiome consists of an astonishing variety of bacteria — estimates suggest between **500 and 1000** distinct bacterial species in a typical healthy adult gut, though this varies considerably between individuals based on diet, environment, and genetics.
>
> What do these bacteria do? Their functions are remarkably varied. They assist with digestion — breaking down complex carbohydrates and fibre that our own enzymes can\'t process. They play a crucial role in immune system **regulation and support** — approximately 70% of the immune system resides in the gut. And they synthesise vitamins, including vitamin K and several B vitamins.
>
> When the normal balance of the microbiome is disrupted — by illness, antibiotic use, or poor diet — the resulting condition is called **dysbiosis**. This imbalance has been associated with a wide range of health conditions.
>
> Antibiotics, while life-saving, can reduce microbiome **diversity** by up to 90% — and in some cases, the microbiome may take months or years to fully recover.
>
> Probiotics are **live and beneficial** bacteria — found in fermented foods like yoghurt, kefir, and sauerkraut — that can help restore microbial balance.
>
> Prebiotics, by contrast, are **dietary fibre** components that specifically feed beneficial gut bacteria, promoting their growth and activity.
>
> The gut-brain axis is a fascinating bidirectional communication system linking the microbiome to **mental and brain** health. Emerging research suggests the microbiome influences mood, cognition, and even risk of depression and anxiety.
>
> We're increasingly linking microbiome imbalance to **inflammation**, obesity, and a growing list of chronic conditions. This is why personalised **microbiome** medicine is considered one of the most promising frontiers in 21st-century healthcare.

>
> Photography was first recognised as a serious art form in the **20th** century, when galleries and museums began to accept photographs alongside paintings and sculpture.

## Section 4 — Lecture: Neuroscience of Memory

> **Listening Passage — Transcript**
>
> Good morning. Today we conclude our series on cognitive science with a deep dive into the neuroscience of memory — how we form, store, and retrieve information, and what happens when these processes break down.
>
> Memory is broadly divided into **short-term or working** memory and long-term memory. Working memory is the system that temporarily holds information in conscious awareness for active processing — it\'s what you use when you're mentally performing arithmetic or holding a phone number in mind.
>
> The capacity of working memory is famously limited. Cognitive psychologist George Miller described it as **seven, plus or minus two** — meaning most people can hold between five and nine items at once in working memory. More recent research suggests the true figure may be closer to four items.
>
> The hippocampus — a seahorse-shaped structure deep in the medial temporal lobe — plays a central role in **encoding or forming** new memories. It acts as a binding agent, integrating information from different sensory systems into a coherent episodic memory.
>
> Damage to the hippocampus causes **anterograde amnesia** — the inability to form new long-term memories. The patient can remember everything from before the damage, but cannot create new memories after it. The famous case of H.M. — who underwent bilateral hippocampal removal in 1953 — remains the most studied case in neuropsychological history.
>
> Long-term memory is divided into **explicit or declarative** memory — which includes episodic and semantic memory — and implicit memory, which covers skills and conditioned responses.
>
> Procedural memory — how to ride a bicycle, how to play a musical instrument — is stored primarily in the **basal ganglia** and cerebellum, explaining why these skills survive hippocampal damage.
>
> Sleep is critical for memory consolidation. Memories are replayed and stabilised during **REM and deep** sleep, which is why sleep deprivation has such dramatic effects on learning and recall.
>
> The "spacing effect" refers to the well-established finding that **spaced** study — distributing study sessions over time — produces far better long-term retention than massed or cramming.
>
> Stress has complex effects on memory. The hormone **cortisol**, released during stress, enhances memory formation for emotionally significant events but can impair the formation of neutral memories.
>
> Finally, promising treatments for memory disorders include **transcranial magnetic and deep brain** stimulation techniques, which are being trialled for conditions including Alzheimer\'s disease.

## Common IELTS Listening Tips

1. **Read questions before listening** — you have 30–45 seconds to preview each section.
2. **Follow the order** — answers usually appear in the same order as the questions.
3. **Watch for distractors** — speakers often mention something and then correct or change it.
4. **Spelling counts** — incorrect spelling means a wrong answer.
5. **Word limit is strict** — "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS" means exactly that.
6. **Listen for signpost language** — words like *firstly*, *however*, *moving on* signal new information.
7. **Plurals matter** — "book" vs "books" can change the meaning.
8. **Numbers and dates** — write digits not words unless instructed (e.g., 14 not fourteen).
9. **Transfer time** — use the 10 minutes (paper test) carefully to check answers.
10. **Practice with native-speed audio** — use BBC, podcasts, and official Cambridge materials.
